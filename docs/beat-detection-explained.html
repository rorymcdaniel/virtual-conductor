Virtual Conductor · A musician’s guideNovember 12, 2026

Learning to listen
more carefully.

The clearest failures were happening before the app could make a useful tempo decision: it was losing some attacks, joining others together, and overlooking quiet playing.

This update repairs that listening stage in both Live following and Record interpretation. Here is what we found, why it mattered musically, and how we checked the improvements.

01 · The musical goal

Find the pace of the phrase.

Your premise is right: the accompaniment needs enough evidence to understand your pace. An occasional wrong or missed note should not prevent that. The score gives the app context: the same sequence of attacks could be quarters, eighths, or triplets, each implying a different tempo.

The app therefore uses recognizable notes in the solo phrase to locate you in the score, then estimates pace from their timing. Beats inside held notes or between attacks are inferred from the surrounding phrase.

Notes provide landmarks. The purpose of recognizing them is to understand where you are and how quickly you are moving through the music.

02 · What was going wrong

Eight notes could become one.

In a repeatable recording test, eight separately articulated Cs were interpreted as one long C. The short gaps between attacks were being smoothed away. That removed the very information needed to estimate the player’s tempo.

Repeated notes blurred together

Recording analysis treated short separations between the same pitch as part of a continuous note.

Changed: keep those articulations so they can serve as timing landmarks.

Fast phrases lost attacks

Live audio sometimes arrived in groups. The app looked only at the end of each group, skipping events in between.

Changed: examine the full sequence and preserve when each event occurred.

Quiet playing disappeared

A fixed loudness cutoff rejected a clean, quiet test phrase before its pitches could be recognized.

Changed: admit quieter musical signals while continuing to check whether the sound has a clear pitch.

03 · What changed

A shared way of listening.

The two features previously used different listening code. They now share the improved analysis, so an improvement in recognizing an attack benefits both. Timing follows the recorded sound itself, rather than the moments when the app happens to check for new audio.

Your playing → recognizable attacks and their timing

One shared listening stage supplies two different jobs.

While you play

Live following

Uses the phrase to estimate your current pace and guide accompaniment within your configured tempo tolerance. Free passages and explicit wait points retain their existing roles.

After you play

Record interpretation

Uses the captured phrase to propose a map of beat times for review and accompaniment playback.

Recovery also matters. If audio delivery is interrupted, the app now resets its timing without treating an already-held note as a new entrance. That protects wait points from an accidental orchestral release.

04 · What the tests tell us

The original failure cases now pass.

I generated musical phrases with known note and beat times, reproduced the failures, and ran the same phrases through the corrected app. The broader checks include repeated notes, legato, quiet playing, vibrato, an occasional wrong note, and simulated headphone leakage.

Within 40 ms

Beat timing in the generated tests

The estimated beat times stayed within four hundredths of a second of the known performance times.

8.3 s → 0.025 s

Substantial processing headroom

The release build analyzed 8.3 seconds of audio in about 0.025 seconds on this Mac. This measures computation, not microphone-to-speaker delay.

The full suite reported 116 tests with zero failures and one opt-in hardware check skipped. A further release run passed 25 focused checks. Review also caught the interruption-recovery issues described above; regression tests now cover them.

The next evidence comes from playing.

These results establish concrete improvements on controlled signals. Real horn attacks, room sound, microphone gain, and headphone bleed can still challenge the listener. Strong pitched background sound and ambiguous phrases remain limitations. Reliability comparable to SmartMusic or Cadenza has not been demonstrated by these tests.
